Part 1 was about **sorting unknown numbers**.

A restaurant has an unknown average quality. A football player has an unknown skill. A candidate model has an unknown benchmark score. The decision may be difficult, but at least its shape is simple: choose one thing, compare two things, or take the best few things.

Part 2 begins when sorting is no longer enough — when the action is a **whole structured object** and you must commit to all of it at once.

Imagine a city with 52 stops. Every morning a driver must leave the depot, visit every stop exactly once, and return home. There is no option to keep only the roads that look cheap. A route is one object: one bad edge can ruin an elegant plan, and one beautiful shortcut is useless if it leaves the rest of the tour impossible.

Now make the map uncertain. Every road has a real average travel time, but the value is hidden. Traffic, weather, loading time, random delays — one trip is only a noisy observation, and you learn the roads you actually drive. The roads you skip stay counterfactual.

> **You do not learn the city at once. You learn the roads you drive.**

That is the seed of a semi-bandit, and it is the shape of a whole family of problems. This part is a toolbox: three of them — a route, a matching, a schedule — each with a live demo, all built on the same move.

## The recurring shape

Across all three, the action decomposes into pieces, and the feedback is **semi-bandit**: you pick a structured action, and reality returns a noisy outcome for each *selected* piece — not the whole answer, not a single aggregate number, but a labelled value per component you used.

That sits between the two familiar extremes:

| After one action, what does the learner see? | |
| --- | --- |
| Full information | every piece's value, even the ones you didn't use |
| Ordinary bandit | one number for the whole action |
| **Semi-bandit** | the realized value of every piece you actually selected |

Because credit is local — you know exactly which road was slow, which booking went well — the learner can update each used piece on its own. But a pile of good pieces is not yet a legal action. Cheap edges can form little loops instead of a tour; high-value bookings can send three clients to one expert. So every round splits into two jobs:

```text
Learning:      estimate the uncertain value of each piece
Optimization:  assemble those values into one valid action
```

That second job is a real solver, and which solver it is depends on the geometry of the action:

> **A semi-bandit is an optimizer wrapped around uncertainty.**

For a top-K list, the solver is sorting. For a route, it is a TSP optimizer. For a client-to-expert allocation, it is a matching/assignment solver. For a weekly calendar, it is a constrained assignment. The learner supplies uncertain component values; the solver enforces the shape of the decision. The feedback pattern stays the same while the geometry changes completely.

The rest of this part walks the same loop three times.

## Example 1 · Routing — learning a city by driving it

Start with six stops. Small enough to inspect, large enough to get into trouble.

Choose the six cheapest roads independently. You get two little triangles. Every stop has two neighbours. The total looks excellent. The driver cannot complete the trip.

A route needs a global constraint: all the stops must belong to **one connected cycle**. The solver enforces that constraint even when an attractive road would tempt us to break it.

<Demo src="/demos/posts/learning-the-map/route-lab.html?v=2" title="Build a route, observe its roads, and watch the map change" height={780} />

**Try it in three steps.** First, press **Try the six cheapest roads**, then build a connected route yourself or let the solver find one. Next, drive it. The demo pauses before observations change the estimates: six roads return travel times; the other nine return nothing. Finally, update the estimates and ask the policy for another plan.

Let the city be a graph $G=(V,E)$ where each edge has an unknown mean cost $\mu_e$. The target is

$$T^* = \arg\min_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{e\in T}\mu_e,$$

where $\mathcal{T}$ contains only valid tours. The solver knows that set. The learner does not know the costs that decide which tour is best.

For six stops there are 15 undirected edges and 60 distinct tours, counting a route and its reversal as the same tour. We can check every tour. That makes the reference optimum exact and removes solver quality as a possible explanation for a policy's performance. A large routing problem would need a more capable solver or an approximation; it would introduce a second source of error.

**Optimism, with the sign flipped.** In a maximization problem, uncertainty makes an option look more promising. Here we minimize, so an exploration bonus becomes a discount:

$$\widetilde{\mu}_{t,e}=\widehat{\mu}_{t,e}-b_{t,e}.$$

The solver finds the best valid tour under those discounted costs. The discount is not a prediction that traffic will be lighter. It is a reason to investigate a road whose cost is still uncertain. In the demo, $b_{t,e}$ grows with elapsed rounds and shrinks as direct observations accumulate; its scale is a teaching heuristic, not a calibrated confidence bound.

The sampling policy asks a different question: *what if this were the true map?* It draws one plausible cost for every road, holds those costs fixed while solving, then drives the resulting route. Resampling a road every time the solver looks at it would give the solver a moving target instead of an imagined city.

Open **Compare policies** and watch both measures. A policy may eventually recommend a good route after spending many days exploring expensive roads. Another may start with a fortunate estimate and need little exploration in this particular world. The experiment should show those possibilities; it should not promise the same winner on every map.

## Example 2 · Matching — pairing clients with experts

Four clients need four experts. Each expert has one appointment.

Look down the starting estimates: everyone wants Mira. Giving each client their highest-scoring expert produces four attractive choices and one impossible afternoon.

The question is now about **opportunity cost**. Giving Mira to Alex means someone else must take their second choice. Who gives up the least? The best complete matching may send a client to an expert who was not first in that client's row.

<Demo src="/demos/posts/learning-the-map/matching-lab.html?v=2" title="Build a matching and see why observing every client is not exploring every pairing" height={780} />

Press **Give everyone their favourite**, then repair the assignment. The numbers are public starting estimates. After you commit, each selected pair returns a noisy outcome. Only then can you improve your knowledge of those pairings.

There are 24 possible one-to-one assignments here, so the demo checks all of them. At larger sizes, the Hungarian algorithm solves the same linear assignment problem without enumerating every possibility. The learner still supplies the values; the assignment solver still enforces one client per expert.

This example exposes a tempting mistake: four observations per round do not mean four **new pairs** per round. A policy can make the same four matches again and again. Every client gets an appointment, every expert stays busy, and twelve alternatives remain untested.

In **Compare policies**, read the **pieces tried** column alongside the quality curves. Trusting the current estimates can work well when the starting assignment is good. It can also preserve an early mistake. A valid assignment does not supply exploration by itself.

## Example 3 · Scheduling — filling a weekly calendar

Now give two experts a three-day calendar. There are six appointments and four clients. Alex cannot attend Wednesday. Bea cannot attend Monday. Cam needs a service available only from Mira. Two clients cannot occupy the same appointment.

A booking has to fit the calendar before its quality matters.

<Demo src="/demos/posts/learning-the-map/schedule-lab.html?v=2" title="Respect a calendar and inspect how one observation changes related booking estimates" height={800} />

Build a feasible week, then reveal its four outcomes. The default learner uses **shared experience**: clients have public types, experts have public specialties, and observations of one type–specialty combination inform other bookings in that group.

Watch what happens when you press **Update the estimates**. Selected bookings gain direct observations. Some unselected predictions change too, because related bookings supplied evidence. Open the evidence table: those rows still have no new direct sample. Their changed estimates are inferences, not measurements.

That distinction is the point of the experiment. We did not book Alex into every slot. We learned something from a similar booking and used it to revise our expectation.

The shared learner starts with a group estimate, then gives a booking's own observations more influence as they accumulate. Sharing can help early; direct evidence lets individual differences matter. The model can still be wrong about which bookings deserve to share information.

Test that assumption in **Compare policies**. Turn **Shared structure** to **None** and rerun the same seeds. The public labels remain, but they no longer predict the hidden residual quality. Compare twelve worlds rather than looking for a pleasing single curve.

You can also remove the client constraints while keeping the same clients, appointments and hidden values. That isolates a different question: how does changing the feasible set affect learning? Constraints may exclude useful probes, but can also remove alternatives that no longer need consideration. The direction is something to inspect, not assume.

## The pattern behind the toolbox

Three actions — a tour, a matching, a schedule — three feasible sets, and one loop:

```text
1. choose a structured action (the solver enforces its shape)
2. observe a value for each piece you used   (semi-bandit feedback)
3. update the uncertain piece-values
4. call the solver again
```

The loop contains two decisions. **Which plan should we use now?** And **how should its feedback change our next estimate?**

The demos separate those decisions:

- **Trust the estimates:** optimize current component means without an exploration adjustment.
- **Explore uncertain pieces:** add an uncertainty bonus for rewards, or subtract one for costs, before solving.
- **Sample a plausible world:** draw a complete set of component values and solve against that fixed draw. The demo uses a Gaussian approximation rather than an exact Bayesian posterior.
- **Share experience + explore:** use related components to inform each estimate, retain per-component observations, and apply an exploration adjustment.

Sharing evidence is a modelling choice. Adding a bonus is a decision policy. Keeping them separate helps us ask where an improvement came from.

### A good plan found is not necessarily a good plan learned

The simulator knows every hidden mean. The learner sees only selected noisy outcomes. That separation matters when we evaluate the experiment.

If we report the best true plan the learner ever visited, we may reward an accident the learner cannot recognize or repeat. Instead, the demos measure two things:

| Measure | Question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| **Quality of learned recommendation** | If exploration stopped now, how good is the plan the learner would choose using its estimated means? |
| **Total cost of learning** | How much expected value did all the executed plans sacrifice relative to the true optimum? |

For costs, the first is the recommended plan's true expected cost minus the optimum. For rewards, the subtraction reverses. The second adds the corresponding gap for every plan actually executed: cumulative pseudo-regret.

A low final gap does not erase the bill paid to get there. And a cheap learning history in one world does not establish that a policy would discover a better option in another. Both views belong in the comparison.

## What these toy models leave out

Each demo keeps one clean assumption: a piece has its own stable noisy value, and the action's value is the sum of its pieces. Reality frays both. Travel times are correlated and depend on time of day and direction; a booking's value depends on price, history, and the rest of the schedule; "value" is often a click conditioned on exposure rather than a clean sample. A production system needs context, correlation, non-stationarity, and counterfactual care.

But the simplification earns its place. It lets the architecture show through without a forecasting stack on top of it:

```text
uncertain piece values  →  optimistic / sampled estimates  →  solver  →  valid action  →  per-piece observations  →  updated model
```

The model changes, the solver changes, the feedback gets messier. The central question does not:

> **How do you commit to a valid plan when the value of its pieces is learned only by using them?**

Part 1 answered it for a restaurant, a duel, a top-K list. Here it is a route, a matching, a calendar. The action keeps getting bigger; the move stays the same — a solver, wrapped around uncertainty.
