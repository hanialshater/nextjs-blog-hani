Wake up, brush your teeth, open LinkedIn. What do you see?

Groundbreaking announcements. Mind-blowing achievements. People absolutely thrilled to share. Everyone is a visionary, every company is transforming something, and apparently the only person having an ordinary Tuesday is you.

You scroll faster. Linear algebra, optimization, deep learning, LLMs, agents, MLOps. Too many papers. Too many people to catch up with. You know what success looks like: dense appendices, Greek letters, architecture diagrams that resemble circuit boards. You recognize the shape before you know whether the thing works.

Let me take you somewhere more honest: an IKEA showroom.

Nobody lives in those rooms. Nobody has left a wet towel on that immaculate chair. Yet you walk through, think “this is how a home should look,” and rearrange your real apartment to match. The staged room is not merely a picture of domestic life. It becomes a template that domestic life tries to obey.

**The representation starts producing the reality it supposedly represents.**

That is the inversion I want to borrow from Jean Baudrillard's *Simulacra and Simulation*. My claim is not that AI is fake. It is that the public image of AI can start deciding which work gets built, which careers count, and which evidence people bother to ask for.

A bubble is a story about prices. This is also a story about how we learn to recognize value. A price correction would not automatically undo it.

## Four ways the sign gets ahead of the work

Baudrillard's phases of the image are useful here as a lens. I am not treating them as four historical periods that began on exact dates. They can coexist in one company, one product launch, even one paper.

### 1. The sign points to something real

A result is attached to a task. Here is the data, the baseline, the procedure, the failure cases. You can ask what improved and how we know.

Real achievements matter to this argument. A useful model gives the story something to grow around. Without that core, there would be less excitement to borrow.

### 2. The sign enlarges what it points to

The result works on a benchmark. The launch says it transforms the industry. The slide says human-level. By the time it reaches LinkedIn, civilization has been replaced and the author is thrilled to have played a small part.

The result may still be good. The problem is the expanding radius of the claim. Every retelling removes a condition: on this dataset, with this setup, for this kind of user, at this cost.

The qualification is not glamorous. It dies first.

### 3. The sign stands in for the missing evidence

You ask whether customers keep using the product. You hear about the funding round. You ask whether the system can complete the task reliably. You get a demo. You ask whether the economics work. You are told the market will be enormous.

Each answer may be true. None answers the question asked.

This is where the substitution becomes hard to see. Money invested is evidence that someone expects value. It is not the same evidence as value already delivered. A successful demonstration shows that something happened under those conditions. It does not tell you how often it happens when the presenter leaves.

### 4. The signs begin validating one another

The company must be important because prominent investors backed it. The investors must be prescient because the company is important. The product must be revolutionary because the talent joined. The talent must be exceptional because they joined the revolutionary company.

You can walk around that circle all day without meeting a customer.

That does not prove the customer is absent. It tells you the argument never needed one. The showroom has learned to sell tickets to the showroom.

## Don't make the critique commit the same mistake

The original version of this essay went too far when it treated the 2024 Nobel Prizes as narrative defeating science. That claim did not survive its own demand for evidence.

The [Physics prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2024/press-release/) recognized Hopfield and Hinton's foundational work enabling machine learning with neural networks. The [Chemistry prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2024/press-release/) recognized Baker's computational protein design and Hassabis and Jumper's work on protein-structure prediction. Those are specific scientific contributions. They do not validate every AI company's roadmap, but neither can I dismiss them because the industry used them as a celebration of itself.

The same discipline applies to finance. Chip revenue is real revenue. A valuation is an expectation about future returns, not a fixed pile of cash taken out of the rest of the economy. Market concentration alone does not yield a mathematical ceiling that proves an inevitable crash.

The defensible questions are about dependence and durability. How much demand would remain without fresh financing? How much value reaches customers outside the investment loop? What happens if expected growth arrives more slowly?

Those are questions to investigate company by company. “It's all recycled money” is another elegant story if I don't do that work.

## Nobody has to be the architect

Before you look for a cabal running the simulation, consider a more irritating possibility: sincere people responding to understandable incentives.

A researcher wants resources. A founder needs a story that can travel. An employee wants a career. An investor needs a return. Each can make a locally sensible choice while the shared picture drifts further from what has actually been demonstrated.

Here is a hypothetical loop: a cloud company invests in a startup; the startup spends part of the funding on compute; that spending supports the cloud company's growth story; the growth story attracts more investment. The compute may perform useful work. The loop is not automatically fraudulent. But counting its financing, spending, and valuation as three independent confirmations of end-user demand would overstate the evidence.

Baudrillard's distinction between use value and sign value helps me name the temptation. Does the thing solve a problem? Or does owning, funding, using, or talking about it certify that I belong among the people who understand the future?

It can do both. That is why the problem is harder than exposing a fake.

## The cynical participant

And here's the really fucked up part: you can notice the performance and still participate perfectly.

You smirk at “thrilled to share.” You complain about complexity theater. Then you update your profile using the language that made you roll your eyes five minutes earlier. The rent is real. The promotion process is real. The people rewarding the performance are real.

**We know it's bullshit. We do it anyway.**

Cynicism can become a comfortable distance from our own behavior. I get to feel above the game while making every move it asks me to make. Even “I see through AI hype” can become a professional costume. Same showroom, darker furniture.

The interesting question is what changes after you notice. If the answer is only your tone, the system has accommodated the critique quite cheaply.

## A correction is possible; inevitability is not an argument

Technical progress can continue while particular business models fail. Useful products can coexist with excessive expectations. Infrastructure can outlast the companies that financed it. A sector can change through a crash, a slow repricing, consolidation, or years of ordinary operational work.

I cannot derive one inevitable path from a philosophical metaphor.

Automation also creates a distribution question: who receives the gains, and what happens to people whose work loses value? It does not follow mechanically that every displaced job destroys the industry's customer base. The effects depend on new work, prices, ownership, institutions, and policy. The spreadsheet that calls every labor cost a future saving is incomplete; so is the essay that treats every saving as economic collapse.

What I expect to remain valuable is the work whose usefulness can survive a less flattering description. A system that saves someone time after error correction and supervision. A method that improves on an honest baseline. A product someone would still choose without the word “AI” on the box.

That is an expectation I can use to guide work. It is not a market forecast dressed as a law of history.

## Living with the showroom

There is no red pill that lets you stop having a career, paying bills, or depending on other people. You still have to navigate the stories. But you can change the questions you ask before giving them your time.

**Ask for the missing comparison.** Better than what, for whom, and at what total cost? A baseline can do more damage to a performance than a thousand sarcastic comments.

**Keep the conditions attached.** When you repeat a result, repeat the setup and the limits. Don't convert a narrow achievement into a universal promise because the shorter sentence travels better.

**Value inspectability.** Open code, documented methods, and accessible artifacts make some claims easier to check. They do not automatically make training data available, reproduction affordable, or an open model correct. Ask what you can actually inspect.

**Choose an outcome that can disagree with you.** A customer returning, an error rate under real load, a learner solving a new problem. Then check that your chosen metric still represents the thing you care about.

The showroom is allowed to inspire you. Just measure the room you actually live in before buying the sofa.
