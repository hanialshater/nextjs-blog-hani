![A four-stage spaceship diagram of the AI hype cycle, from reproducible results to a self-sustaining narrative](/static/images/posts/welcome-to-the-greatest-hallucination/simulacra.png)

Wake up in the morning, brush your teeth, open LinkedIn. Tell me, what do you see?

A stream of groundbreaking announcements. "Experts" absolutely thrilled to share. Everyone is founding something, shipping something, or explaining why the rest of us have eighteen months left to remain employable.

Everyone except you.

You have a backlog. Linear algebra, optimization, deep learning, LLMs, agents, MLOps. Papers to read, tools to learn, people to catch up to. You were halfway through understanding yesterday's future when somebody released tomorrow's.

And you know what success looks like. A very smart person writes complex math, attaches an appendix full of Greek letters, and draws an architecture diagram that needs its own architecture diagram. We've learned to recognize the shape.

But how sure are you that this is what success looks like? You haven't lived it. You've only seen the showroom.

So let me take you to an actual showroom. IKEA. You walk through rooms nobody lives in, think _look how good that looks_, then go home and move your furniture. The staged room isn't a copy of a real room. It's the template for one. The fake produces the real.

Careers work the same way. The public performance of success becomes the spec for how to live: which skills, which title, even which problems will make your life look like the picture.

Companies do it too. A board asks what a company fit for the future looks like. The answer comes from other companies' announcements. A roadmap follows, then a team, then a budget. By the next funding round, those commitments are presented as evidence that the future is arriving.

Jean Baudrillard had a name for this: the simulacrum. A representation that stops pointing at reality and starts giving it orders.

The showroom starts issuing instructions.

In AI, it does this at a scale Baudrillard would have enjoyed. Expectations attract investment. Investment buys infrastructure and becomes revenue for suppliers. The revenue becomes evidence for the expectations. A useful product can survive all of this and still earn a fraction of what the business built around it needs.

I'll come back to the money. First, one person.

## The Programmer Behind the App

Someone builds their first serious application by vibe coding. They describe what they want, ask the model for changes, and keep going until it runs. Login screen, database, payment flow. People can use it. Genuinely impressive.

Then an order shows up twice. They ask the model to fix it. It writes a patch. The duplicate disappears. They can't tell you what caused it, or why the patch works.

We'd call this forgetting how to code. But there was nothing to forget. They never made the implementation decisions we assume a finished app demonstrates.

I'm not mocking this. Getting it built is real, and directing a model well is a real skill. But the app can't tell anyone whether its maker can reason about the code on the day the model's next answer is wrong.

Then the app goes into a portfolio, and it starts standing in for a history of understanding its maker never had. The employer reads it that way. After a while, the maker does too.

That's the hallucination I find interesting. Not the model inventing a citation. Us, inventing a person behind a working product. The code is there for anyone to inspect. The competence we attached to its author, nobody checked.

## How the Story Takes Over

Baudrillard described four phases of the image, each further from the thing it once pointed at. They're a good lens for watching an AI announcement drift. In real life the phases overlap: the same company can do excellent research in the morning and sell a future it can't deliver by lunch.

### Phase 1: The sign reflects reality

A model does better at a task. Other people inspect the method, rerun it, argue about it. The claim has somewhere to answer for itself.

In 2011, IBM's Watson [beat two _Jeopardy!_ champions](https://research.ibm.com/publications/introduction-to-this-is-watson). Rules, opponents, a result you could watch on TV. A real achievement in answering natural-language questions. The distance between the announcement and the result was short enough to walk.

### Phase 2: The sign distorts reality

A team tries forty-seven architectures, publishes the winner, and lets the other forty-six rest in peace. The result is real. The presentation hides how much searching it took, which baselines were convenient, and what happens off the benchmark.

Then the result travels. The paper shows a capability under particular conditions. The keynote drops the conditions. The sales deck turns the capability into a product. By the time it reaches the customer, the model has picked up several skills its authors never tested.

Nobody had to lie. Everyone just made the story a little easier to tell.

### Phase 3: The sign masks absence

Now the promise jumps from a demonstrated task to an anticipated capability.

It can write code; the pitch becomes autonomous software engineering. It can answer questions about your documents; the pitch becomes an employee who understands your business. The gap between the two holds research problems, integration work, and occasionally a human quietly keeping the demo alive.

This is where AGI becomes commercially useful before anyone agrees how to recognize it. It gives wildly different investments the same destination.

In January 2025, Stargate [announced it intended to invest USD 500 billion](https://openai.com/index/announcing-the-stargate-project/) in AI infrastructure over four years. Half a trillion dollars. The number is so big it glitches. And then it starts working as an argument on its own: surely nobody would plan to spend that much unless the destination were close.

The size of the bet becomes evidence for the bet.

### Phase 4: The signs refer to each other

Eventually the announcements stop pointing at results and start pointing at each other.

The startup must have something, because a giant invested. The investment must be smart, because demand for compute is exploding. The demand must prove customer value, because startups keep buying compute.

Here's how that gets wired. In January 2025 the FTC [published a study](https://www.ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/tech-at-ftc/2025/01/behind-ftcs-6b-report-large-ai-partnerships-investments) of three partnerships: Microsoft and OpenAI, Amazon and Anthropic, Google and Anthropic. More than USD 20 billion in investment between them. In each, the AI developer had agreed to spend a significant, set amount of that investment money on cloud services from the investor.

Follow the money for a second. The investor puts money in. The startup announces its round. The money flows back to the investor as cloud spending, and the investor books cloud revenue. Two impressive headlines, one financing decision.

The services are real. The revenue is real. Funding work before customers can pay for it is literally what investment is for. The trick is in how we read it: the funding announcement and the cloud revenue look like two independent signs that customers will eventually pay. They're partly the same sign, counted twice.

If the paying customers arrive, the loop built something. If they don't, the next funding round has to keep the demand looking alive.

It's turtles all the way down, and the turtles have a board deck.

The same circle runs inside companies. Why do we need an AI strategy? Because every serious company has one. How do we know serious companies need one? Look at all the strategies.

At this point the loop sets its own admission criteria. A project that fits the picture of the future looks sensible before anyone asks whose problem it solves. A useful project outside the picture has to explain why it exists.

## Watson Goes to the Clinic

Remember Watson on _Jeopardy!_? Two years later it was being prepared for a very different stage.

In November 2013, MD Anderson [told its readers](https://www.mdanderson.org/cancerwise/what-ibm-watson-means-for-our-patients.h00-158834379.html) that its Watson-powered Oncology Expert Advisor would soon be in the hands of doctors and researchers, starting with leukemia. The ambition stretched to every clinic, and to bringing specialist knowledge beyond Houston. Further down the same article, the hospital said the system was still being tested and nobody knew when patients would get it.

Then the paperwork. The University of Texas System's fiscal 2017 audit report [summarized a procurement review](https://www.utsystem.edu/sites/default/files/offices/system-audit/SAO%20Annual%20Report%20FY%202017%20-%20UT%20System%20Audit%20Office%20-%20FINAL.pdf#page=20): MD Anderson's standard procedures were adequate, but they hadn't been consistently applied to this project. The 2018 audited financial statements [recorded a USD 26.5 million impairment](https://utsystem.edu/sites/default/files/documents/report-state/2018/consolidated-annual-financial-report-fy-2018/ut-system-audit-afr-2018.pdf#page=86) on part of the project in 2017. That's a write-down of the asset's value, not the total spent. The project was still listed as construction in progress.

I want to be careful here, because this is a real hospital trying to help real patients. Ambitious projects are allowed to fail. The records show a financial disappointment and controls that weren't followed. They don't prove the hype caused either.

But look at the arc. The same system, two years apart. On _Jeopardy!_, the sign pointed at a result you could watch. In the clinic, the sign pointed at a future, and the future was doing the persuading. The showroom had become a project, and the project still had to pass inspection.

## The Nature of the Simulacrum

Before you go looking for the architect, some shadowy cabal pulling strings, let me save you the time. There isn't one. **The Matrix wasn't built by machines. It emerged from us**: ordinary people trying to get their next decision approved.

Fear of being left out. Need for status. Watching the tribe to know where it's safe to stand. These instincts kept us alive in the stone age. Now they make us chase metrics we don't believe in.

In Baudrillard's terms, every product carries sign value as well as use value. A tool can signal that a company is innovative while contributing almost nothing to how it runs. A complicated architecture can make a team look sophisticated while making the service harder to keep alive.

Both values can live together. The trouble starts when the rewards for the appearance get strong enough to switch off the checks on the substance.

## We Know It's Bullshit. We Do It Anyway.

You know all this already. You roll your eyes at the announcements. You can spot the demo with a person behind the curtain.

Then your manager asks about the AI roadmap.

Your competitors have one. The board expects one. Your engineers want experience that still looks current on a CV next year. The founder down the street has to close a round before payroll. Everyone has a reason to play, even when they privately doubt the show.

This is the part I can't shake. The system doesn't need anyone to believe. It runs perfectly well on people who think they see through it and can manage their exposure. Peter Sloterdijk called this enlightened false consciousness. Žižek's version is shorter: they know very well what they are doing, but still, they are doing it.

We laugh, update our profiles, and carry on.

And here's what makes it harder to write off: inside the performance, people are doing excellent work. The incentives don't sort us neatly into honest builders and dishonest narrators. The same person solves a hard problem in the morning and oversells it in the afternoon. I've been that person.

## The Glitches

The real test comes after the demo, when someone has to do the job with the tool. Some tools pass it beautifully.

In a field study of 5,172 customer-support agents, AI assistance [raised issues resolved per hour by 15% on average](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658), and more for less experienced agents. That's not hype. That's worth getting excited about.

Now watch what the story does with it. The finding becomes a forecast about replacing knowledge work. The forecast justifies a valuation. The valuation attracts investment. The investment gets cited as proof the forecast was sensible. A 15% improvement for one support team is now carrying a family of claims it never signed up for.

So I watch for glitches closer to the business. A pilot that never expands. Time saved drafting that reappears as time spent checking. Usage going up while the provider loses more money serving it. Impressive revenue from customers funded by the provider's own investors.

And I watch what happens when results disappoint. Does the explanation get more specific: a better test, a different product? Or does the payoff just move another eighteen months out?

## Where the Loop Can Break

A lab misses its revenue target and needs more funding to keep buying compute. The round doesn't close. It cuts its cloud spending. The cloud provider loses some of the growth that justified investing in the lab.

The loop can run backwards.

That doesn't have to look like a crash. It can look like slower construction, quiet acquisitions, and returns that disappoint investors while the products keep getting better. A long, expensive disappointment sends invoices too.

Three pressure points decide which version we get.

**Someone has to pay.** Customers need to capture enough value to pay for the service, and providers need to keep enough of that payment to cover the full cost of running it and financing the data centers. Competition could make powerful AI cheap and everywhere while investors lose money. Customers would call that progress. Shareholders would choose another word.

**Someone has to have a job.** If the savings go to owners while displaced workers lose their income, the customer base starts shrinking. Lower prices, new kinds of work, or redistribution could offset that. No single company's plan to cut its own wage bill settles the question.

**Someone has to discover something.** A company can approve a bigger training run. It can't approve the discovery that run is supposed to produce. When the next leap takes longer than the funding cycle, the research problem gets a deadline and the researcher gets a performance review.

## What Would Change My Mind

Thinking in public means showing where you moved.

The first version of this essay, in November 2025, had a section called "The Inevitable Collapse." I was sure. Economics, automation, and science would all hit the wall, and "the question isn't _whether_ there's a correction. It's how severe."

I took the word out. Not because I got more optimistic, but because "inevitable" was doing exactly what I was criticizing. A theory that explains every possible outcome stops being a theory and becomes a way to protect its narrator. Calling everything a simulacrum shouldn't give me that privilege.

So here is what would change my mind. Growing numbers of customers renewing at prices that cover the full cost of the service. Adoption that survives the end of discounts and subsidies. Measured benefits that hold up in normal operation, not just in pilots. If that cash can carry the infrastructure, the next funding round stops being a life-or-death event, and the loop has built something real.

And here is what would deepen my worry: usage held up by discounts, pilots that never turn into routine purchases, and obligations refinanced again and again while the payoff keeps moving away.

I'll keep checking. You should too.

## The Reload

Say part of the financial story breaks. Useful technology survives corrections. The people who built it can still lose their jobs. The data centers get new owners, sometimes owners with more power than before.

A crash doesn't hand the machines to the most deserving engineer.

The dot-com crash didn't make the internet less useful. It exposed the distance between a technology becoming important and a particular investment paying off. An industry can transform the world and still bankrupt the people who correctly saw it coming.

And a reset doesn't switch off the social machinery. The appetite for prestige, the fear of missing out, the hunger for an impressive future: they'll find new material. Every reload plants the seed of the next simulation.

There's no clean normal waiting under the market chart. There are still better ways to build.

## Surviving Simulacra

_The Matrix_ sold a wonderfully convenient product: one pill, permanent clarity. Baudrillard himself said the film misunderstood him, which feels about right. There's no red pill. You have bills, a career, and work worth doing inside the thing. So take both: live in the simulation, think outside it.

Here's how I try to make a story answer to the work, before it grows a department that depends on keeping the story alive.

**Pick projects by following a customer's actual task.** What got easier? What still needs a human to check it? Count integration, review, and failure in the cost. A customer who renews from an operating budget tells you more than any conference applause meter.

**Build on what you can inspect, reproduce, and replace.** Open code and open weights give more people that power. Then count the dependencies that remain: chips, compute, expertise, maintenance. Independence needs an actual budget.

**Give bad news somewhere to go.** A team should be able to say "the pilot disappointed" or "the boring method won" without its survival depending on a more exciting explanation. Otherwise everyone learns which results are welcome.

**Write down the promise.** What was supposed to improve, by how much, under which conditions? Decide what would make you stop before the quarterly review hands you reasons to continue.

You can enjoy the technology. You can build ambitious things with it. You can even be thrilled to share.

Just come back a few months later and share what happened.
